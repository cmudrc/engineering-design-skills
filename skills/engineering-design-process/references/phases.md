# Phase guide

The framework follows Karl T. Ulrich and Steven D. Eppinger, *Product Design and Development*, 6th edition (2016), Chapter 2, pp. 13–16, Exhibits 2-2 and 2-3. Exhibit 1-4 on p. 9 illustrates overlapping method application. The guidance below is an original summary and adaptation for agents.

## 0: Planning

Define the opportunity, intended users, strategic or business aims, and development scope. Examine technology and production constraints early. The useful handoff is a mission statement with assumptions and constraints, rather than a prematurely selected product solution. Revisit planning when the opportunity or project mission materially changes.

## 1: Concept Development

Develop customer needs and target specifications; generate, select, and test concepts; revise specifications as feasibility becomes clearer; and plan downstream work. Competitive benchmarking, economic analysis, and exploratory models or prototypes support these activities throughout. The handoff should explain the selected concept and the evidence behind it. Concept testing is part of this phase.

## 2: System-Level Design

Develop the architecture and allocate functions and requirements to subsystems. Resolve major interfaces, layout choices, and preliminary assembly or production arrangements. A concept sketch alone does not settle interfaces. The handoff includes a system layout, subsystem responsibilities, and interface definitions.

## 3: Detail Design

Specify actual part geometry, materials, tolerances, purchased components, fabrication, and assembly details. Link these choices to the requirements and analysis supporting them. The handoff should be detailed enough for the intended build or procurement activity. A symbolic design optimization model can support this work but does not constitute the complete design definition.

## 4: Testing and Refinement

Evaluate developed product versions against intended needs, operating conditions, performance, reliability, and relevant requirements. Distinguish exploratory concept tests from tests of production-intent product versions. Use findings to revise details, architecture, or even the original framing when warranted. Track unresolved issues and the evidence for each change.

## 5: Production Ramp-Up

Use the intended production system to make and evaluate early output. Assess operator preparation, assembly execution, quality checks, process variation, and remaining production problems. Treat readiness as an evidence-based judgment. A laboratory prototype does not establish the readiness of a production system.

## Membership and iteration

Skills have many-to-many phase membership. Their use depends on the decision: for example, benchmarking may inform opportunity selection in Phase 0 or product-performance comparisons in Phase 4. Record the relevant workstream and expected phase output, and allow feedback to earlier phases. Membership guides discovery; it is not a prohibition on reuse in another justified context.
